# Related work

Retrieval is the usual answer to hallucination, but it helps less reliably than we tend to assume. [Mallen et al. 2022](owlit://library/arxiv-2212.10511) show that retrieval pays off mostly for long-tail facts, and that retrieving only when the model's own knowledge is likely to be weak can beat retrieving every time. [Asai et al. 2023](owlit://library/arxiv-2310.11511) move that decision into the model, which learns when to retrieve and emits reflection tokens that judge whether each passage is relevant and whether its own output is supported. Retrieved text can also mislead, though. [Wu et al. 2024](owlit://library/arxiv-2404.10198) find that models often adopt a wrong document over a correct prior answer, and do so less as the prior grows more confident.

A second line of work checks the answer after it is written. [Manakul et al. 2023](owlit://library/arxiv-2303.08896) need nothing but the model's own samples: a sentence that the other samples do not support is probably made up. For RAG answers, [Kovács & Recski 2025](owlit://library/arxiv-2502.17125) fine-tune a ModernBERT token classifier on RAGTruth to tag the spans an answer's context does not support, while [Sun et al. 2024](owlit://library/arxiv-2410.11414) look inside the model, weighing how much the attention heads draw on the retrieved context against how much the later FFN layers add from parametric memory.

Evaluation frameworks turn the same question into a score. [Es et al. 2023](owlit://library/arxiv-2309.15217) prompt an LLM to rate faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance without reference answers, and [Saad-Falcon et al. 2023](owlit://library/arxiv-2311.09476) fine-tune small judges on synthetic data, then correct their estimates with a few hundred human labels through prediction-powered inference. What I have not found is a comparison across these families on the same RAG outputs: we do not know whether a learned critique token, a sampling-consistency check and an internal-signal detector flag the same claims.
